Born into the illustrious patrician Claudii family, he altered the spelling of his name to "Clodius," closer to popular speech, to bolster his image as a champion of the plebs. His career was marked as early as 62 BC by the Bona Dea scandal, in which he was accused of infiltrating, disguised as a woman, a religious ceremony reserved for Roman women; his scandalous acquittal owed much to jury bribery, and Cicero's testimony against him began a lasting enmity between the two men.

Elected tribune of the plebs in 58 BC after arranging his own adoption by a plebeian, he passed major popular legislation, including free grain distributions to poor citizens, and secured Cicero's exile from Rome. He relied on armed gangs that controlled part of Roman political life through intimidation, in direct rivalry with the gangs of his enemy Milo.

His recurring clashes with Milo culminated on 18 January 52 BC, when the two men crossed paths by chance on the Appian Way: a brawl broke out between their armed escorts, and Clodius was killed. His death triggered rioting in Rome, where the crowd burned his body inside the Senate house, setting the building on fire.
