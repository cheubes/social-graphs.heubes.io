Born into an impoverished branch of the patrician Cornelii, Lucius Cornelius Sulla first made his name as an officer under Gaius Marius during the Jugurthine War, where he was credited with capturing the Numidian king while still a mere quaestor, sowing a lasting rivalry with his former commander. His success in the Social War earned him the Grass Crown and the consulship of 88 BC.

Stripped by a tribune of his command against Mithridates in favour of Marius, Sulla marched on Rome at the head of his legions, the first Roman ever to do so, before departing to fight the First Mithridatic War in the East. On his return he crushed the Marian faction in a fresh civil war, had himself named dictator in 82 BC, and posted proscription lists that condemned thousands of his opponents. His constitutional reforms strengthened the Senate and durably weakened the plebeian tribunate.

Against all expectation, he voluntarily resigned the dictatorship in 79 BC and retired to Puteoli, where he died the following year. The young Pompey, who owed him the nickname "Magnus" after raising an army in his service, was among the officers he trained; Sulla's march on Rome, his dictatorship and his proscriptions would stand, for the generation of Caesar and Pompey, as both a model of personal power and a warning of where it led.
