Great-grandson of Cato the Elder, whose moral legacy he claimed as his own, Marcus Porcius Cato built an early reputation for unbending integrity, refusing the compromises Roman politics usually demanded. A scrupulous quaestor, he cleaned up the management of the public treasury; as tribune of the plebs in 62 BC, he established himself as the uncompromising voice of the senatorial party.

A fierce opponent of the First Triumvirate, he repeatedly obstructed Caesar's agrarian laws in 59 BC, which earned him removal from Rome through a governorship of Cyprus that he carried out with notable probity. He eventually drew closer to Pompey, whom he had nonetheless spent years opposing, hoping to present a united front against Caesar as civil war approached.

After the defeat at Pharsalus, he continued the fight in Africa, organising Pompeian resistance at Utica. Refusing Caesar's clemency after the defeat at Thapsus in 46 BC, he took his own life, reportedly while reading Plato's Phaedo on the immortality of the soul, turning his suicide into a political act that would long inspire opposition to tyranny.
