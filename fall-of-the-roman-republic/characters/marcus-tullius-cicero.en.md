Born into an equestrian family from Arpinum with no consular ancestors, Marcus Tullius Cicero rose on the strength of his oratory alone, a brilliant advocate who climbed the cursus honorum step by step to the consulship of 63 BC. That year he uncovered the Catilinarian conspiracy and had its accomplices executed without trial, a decisive stroke that earned him the title "father of the fatherland" but also the lasting enmity of Clodius, who secured his temporary exile in 58 BC.

Drawn to the defence of republican institutions first out of caution and then out of conviction, Cicero maintained a complicated relationship with the great men of his age: close to Pompey, whom he regarded as the bulwark of legality, wary of Caesar, whose alliance he repeatedly declined, and close to his younger brother Quintus, who served as his legate during his governorship of Cilicia in 51-50 BC. He sided with Pompey at the outbreak of civil war, before being pardoned by Caesar after Pharsalus.

Caesar's assassination in 44 BC brought him back to the forefront: in a series of blistering speeches, the Philippics, he attacked Mark Antony, hoping to see him sidelined in favour of the young Octavian. The gamble backfired when Antony and Octavian allied within the Second Triumvirate: proscribed, Cicero was caught and executed in December 43 BC.
