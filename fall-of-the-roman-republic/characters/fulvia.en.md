Descended on her mother's side from an illustrious line reaching back to the Gracchi and Scipio Africanus, Fulvia belonged to a wealthy plebeian family and married three times into major figures of the popular faction: Clodius Pulcher, killed in 52 BC, then Curio, who died in battle in 49 BC, and finally Mark Antony around 47-46 BC, by whom she had two sons, Antyllus and Iullus Antonius. Each marriage placed her at the heart of Roman political life, where she was actively involved, including through the networks of popular support inherited from Clodius.

When Clodius was killed by Milo's men on the Appian Way in 52 BC, she publicly displayed his bloodied body to rouse the Roman crowd and testified vehemently at his killer's trial. Eleven years later, in 43 BC, tradition, likely hostile and exaggerated, credits her with a grim act: she is said to have pierced the tongue of Cicero's severed head, then displayed on the Rostra at Antony's request, with hairpins, in revenge for the Philippics he had delivered against her husband.

During the Second Triumvirate, while Antony was in the East, Fulvia wielded considerable influence in Rome and did not hesitate to openly oppose Octavian. In 41-40 BC, she allied with Antony's brother, Lucius Antonius, against Octavian and raised several legions in Italy: this conflict, known as the Perusine War, ended with the surrender of Perusia and her exile to Greece. Having taken refuge in Sicyon, near Corinth, she died there in 40 BC of an illness, without ever seeing Antony again, who partly blamed her for this political failure; she remains the first Roman woman to appear on coinage during her own lifetime.
