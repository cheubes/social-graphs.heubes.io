Born Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, he was adopted as an adult by Quintus Caecilius Metellus Pius and took his name, thereby uniting the legacy of two of the greatest families of the Roman nobility. His career followed the standard cursus honorum up to a consulship in 52 BC alongside Pompey, whose father-in-law he became through the marriage of his daughter Cornelia Metella.

This family tie sealed his commitment to the Pompeian party once civil war broke out. As governor of Syria, he raised legions in the East and commanded the centre of the Pompeian line at the Battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC. After the defeat, he continued the fight in Africa, where his kinship with the Scipios, one of whom had once defeated Hannibal, was promoted as a favourable omen for the senatorial cause.

Defeated in turn at the Battle of Thapsus in 46 BC, he tried to escape by sea but, on the point of being captured by Caesar's ships, took his own life. The historian Ronald Syme would call him "the last Scipio of any consequence in Roman history".
