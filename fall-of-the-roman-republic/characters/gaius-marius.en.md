Born into an equestrian family near Arpinum, he was very much a "new man" in a Roman nobility largely closed to provincials. His military career, already outstanding at the siege of Numantia, brought him to a first consulship in 107 BC, during which he concluded the Jugurthine War and thoroughly reformed the recruitment of the Roman army, opening it to landless citizens and creating a professional force whose loyalty ran to its general rather than to the state.

Victor over the Teutones at Aix (102 BC) and then the Cimbri at Vercellae (101 BC), he saved Rome from invasion and went on to hold the consulship a record seven times. As the acknowledged leader of the popular faction, his rivalry with Sulla, leader of the optimates, degenerated after 88 BC into open civil war, marked by successive seizures of Rome and bloody proscriptions on both sides.

Restored to power alongside Cinna in 87 BC, he was elected to a seventh consulship in 86 BC but died of illness only days after taking office. As Julius Caesar's uncle by marriage, whose potential he had recognised early, he is remembered in Roman history as Sulla's great rival and the unwitting architect of the crisis that hastened the Republic's fall.
