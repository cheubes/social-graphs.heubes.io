A native of Picenum like Pompey, whose family his own may have been clients of, Titus Labienus first made his name as tribune of the plebs in 63 BC, the year he restored popular election of the pontifices, a reform that then benefited Caesar.

The lieutenant most consistently named in Caesar's Commentaries, he took part in every campaign of the Gallic Wars: he commanded entire legions on his own, won decisive victories against the Treveri and the Parisii, and held firm during the general uprising led by Vercingetorix. This unwavering trust lasted nearly nine years, making him Caesar's undisputed second-in-command on the Gallic front.

Yet when Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, Labienus made a stunning reversal and joined Pompey's camp, the only senior Caesarian officer to do so. The reasons for this break remain debated: thwarted personal ambition, an older loyalty to Pompey, or a genuine disagreement over the legitimacy of the civil war. He fought without great success at Pharsalus and then at Thapsus, before dying at the Battle of Munda in 45 BC alongside Pompey's sons, in the last gasp of Pompeian resistance.
