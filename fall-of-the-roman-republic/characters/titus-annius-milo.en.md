Titus Annius Milo made his name through the systematic use of armed gangs of gladiators and slaves, deployed in the service of his political ambitions and his alliance with Pompey and the senatorial party. As tribune of the plebs in 57 BC, he worked to recall Cicero from exile, before engaging in near-continuous street warfare against the rival gangs of Publius Clodius Pulcher, Caesar's man in Rome.

This confrontation culminated on 18 January 52 BC on the Appian Way, near Bovillae: a brawl between the two armed retinues escalated, Clodius was wounded and then finished off on Milo's orders at a nearby inn. The riot that followed in Rome, where the mob burned Clodius's body inside the Senate House itself, hastened Pompey's election as sole consul, tasked with restoring order.

Prosecuted for the killing, Milo was unsuccessfully defended by Cicero, whose speech Pro Milone, reworked after the trial, would become a classic of judicial oratory. Convicted, he went into exile at Massilia. In 48 BC he joined a revolt led by Marcus Caelius Rufus against Caesar in Italy and died at the siege of Compsa, struck by a stone thrown from the walls.
