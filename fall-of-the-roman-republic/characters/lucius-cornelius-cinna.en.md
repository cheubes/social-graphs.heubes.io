A patrician by birth, he emerged as one of the leaders of the popular faction alongside Marius during Rome's first civil war. Elected consul in 87 BC, he clashed with his colleague Octavius over the status of Rome's new Italian citizens and was driven from the city by force; he returned at the head of an army, seized Rome together with Marius, and had many of their optimate opponents killed.

Master of Rome after Marius's death, he held the consulship without interruption until 84 BC, an unprecedented arrangement that let him govern on the strength of the Marian party. It was during this period that he married his daughter Cornelia to the young Julius Caesar, then barely seventeen, sealing Caesar's allegiance to the popular cause.

While preparing an expedition against Sulla, who was returning victorious from the East, he was killed in 84 BC in a mutiny by his own troops at Ancona, who refused to embark to face his rival's legions.
