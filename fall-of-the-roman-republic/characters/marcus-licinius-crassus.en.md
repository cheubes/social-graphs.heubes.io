Born into a consular family, he had to rebuild his fortune after Marius's proscriptions cost his father and brother their lives, doing so through Roman real-estate speculation and the exploitation of mines and slaves, until he became the richest man in Rome. He distinguished himself militarily in 71 BC by crushing the revolt of Spartacus, a success partly overshadowed by Pompey's rival glory.

Consul for the first time in 70 BC alongside that same Pompey, despite an enduring rivalry between the two men, he acted as a financial and political broker in Roman public life, notably lending money to the young Caesar to help launch his career. In 60 BC he joined Caesar and Pompey to form the First Triumvirate, then secured a second consulship in 55 BC along with the governorship of the wealthy province of Syria.

Eager to match the military glory of his two partners, he launched an invasion of the Parthian empire in 53 BC that turned into disaster at the Battle of Carrhae, where his son Publius was killed. Forced to negotiate with the Parthian general Surena, he was killed under murky circumstances, bringing the triumvirate to an end and leaving Caesar and Pompey facing each other alone.
