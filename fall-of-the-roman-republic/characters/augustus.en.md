Born Gaius Octavius, he was adopted by will by his great-uncle Julius Caesar and took the name Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus (Octavian). When Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC, he was only eighteen, yet he immediately rallied the dictator's veterans and had himself elected consul in 43 BC after marching on Rome.

Reconciled with Mark Antony after a brief rivalry, he formed the Second Triumvirate with him and Lepidus, which crushed Caesar's assassins at Philippi in 42 BC. Entrusted with the West, Octavian faced the resettlement of veterans and Sextus Pompey's naval resistance, while his alliance with Antony deteriorated as the latter grew closer to Cleopatra. The conflict ended with the naval victory at Actium in 31 BC, won thanks to his friend and general Marcus Agrippa, followed by the conquest of Egypt.

In 27 BC, Octavian formally restored his extraordinary powers to the Senate, which promptly returned them in a new form and granted him the title Augustus. As Rome's first emperor under a carefully maintained republican façade, he ushered in four decades of internal peace, the Pax Romana, and died in AD 14 after arranging his succession.
