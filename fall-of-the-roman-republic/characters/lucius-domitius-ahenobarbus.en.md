A fierce opponent of Caesar from the 60s BC onward, Ahenobarbus made repeated attempts to prevent him from obtaining, and later from keeping, the governorship of Gaul, even putting himself forward as his successor before the legal end of his command. His marriage to Porcia, sister of Cato the Younger, anchored him within the most uncompromising circle of the senatorial party; he reached the consulship in 54 BC.

At the outbreak of civil war in 49 BC, he took it upon himself to defend Corfinium with some thirty cohorts against Caesar's advance, without waiting for the reinforcements Pompey had promised. Besieged for a week, he was eventually handed over by his own soldiers and pardoned by Caesar, a clemency that contrasted with his own intransigence but that Ahenobarbus refused to repay in kind: he immediately took up arms again, defending Massilia against Caesar's forces before joining Pompey in the East.

He commanded the Pompeian right wing at the Battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC and died shortly after the defeat, killed while trying to flee. His son, also nicknamed Ahenobarbus, would continue the fight against Caesar's heirs until the very end of the Second Triumvirate.
