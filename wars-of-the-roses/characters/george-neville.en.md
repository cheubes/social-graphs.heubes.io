The youngest son of the Earl of Salisbury, George Neville owed his meteoric rise through the church as much to his name as to his talents, in an institution where great noble families readily placed their younger sons: Bishop of Exeter at barely twenty-three, a papal dispensation having been required on account of his youth, he thereafter followed his elder brothers Warwick and Montagu's political careers closely, placing his moral authority and connections at the Yorkist cause's service.

Appointed Chancellor of England by Edward IV in 1460, an office that made him keeper of the great seal and one of the kingdom's foremost officers, he held the post until 1467, when Edward IV, already at odds with Warwick, abruptly dismissed him, a move the Nevilles took as an early warning sign of the rupture to come. Elevated shortly afterwards to the archiepiscopal see of York, the second-ranking ecclesiastical dignity in the kingdom after Canterbury, he officiated in 1469 at his niece Isabel's marriage to George of Clarence, personally sealing his family's alliance against the king.

Loyal to Warwick through his rebellion and then his Lancastrian about-face, he was briefly imprisoned in the Tower of London after the defeat at Barnet in 1471, before being released by an Edward IV who preferred not to permanently alienate the Church. A second arrest followed in 1472, on suspicion of a supposed plot no solid evidence ever substantiated, and this time stripped him of all his valuable possessions, books and liturgical ornaments included. Permanently sidelined from political life and deprived of his main income, he died in 1476, ruined, in a relative poverty his ecclesiastical rank made particularly unusual.
