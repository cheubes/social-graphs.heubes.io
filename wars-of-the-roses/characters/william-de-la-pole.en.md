Descended from a merchant family ennobled two generations earlier, William de la Pole first distinguished himself as a captain in the Hundred Years' War, fighting for nearly seventeen years before becoming, on the Duke of Bedford's death in 1435, one of the chief architects of English policy in France. Convinced that continuing the war was no longer sustainable, he worked to negotiate a lasting peace with Charles VII.

This policy of conciliation culminated in 1445 in Henry VI's marriage to Margaret of Anjou, which Suffolk negotiated in person and for which he escorted the young bride to England. The agreement contained a secret clause, the cession of Maine to France, which only came to light later and caused a resounding scandal. Having meanwhile become the kingdom's leading minister and Duke of Suffolk, he drew onto himself all the resentment of a nobility humiliated by military setbacks and by the corruption attributed to his administration.

In 1450, the fall of Normandy precipitated his downfall: impeached by the Commons for treason and embezzlement, he narrowly escaped conviction through Henry VI's personal intervention, which commuted the sentence to five years' banishment. But his ship, en route into exile, was intercepted in the Channel by the crew of a warship, the Nicholas of the Tower: given a summary trial aboard, he was beheaded and his body thrown onto the beach at Dover. This extrajudicial killing of a former minister, which went unpunished, illustrates the collapse of royal authority barely five years before the outbreak of open civil war.
