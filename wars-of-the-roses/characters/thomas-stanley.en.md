Master of vast estates in Lancashire and Cheshire, where his family had exercised near-sovereign authority for several generations, Thomas Stanley inherited in 1459 a domain that let him raise considerable troops at will without depending entirely on royal favour. This material independence fed an almost legendary political caution: throughout the 1450s and 1460s he carefully avoided committing decisively to the clashes between York and Lancaster, offering only measured support to whichever crown was in power.

He thus served Henry VI and then Edward IV in turn without ever attaching himself too openly to either side, a strategy that earned him some contemporaries' distrust but let him weather every reversal of fortune without ever suffering lasting disgrace. His marriage around 1472 to Margaret Beaufort, mother of the exiled Lancastrian claimant Henry Tudor, nonetheless placed him in a particularly delicate position under Richard III's reign, who kept him at court while well aware of his marriage tie to his most dangerous adversary.

After his wife's involvement in Buckingham's 1483 rebellion, Richard III tasked him, as a lenient punishment, with keeping her under watch, a duty Stanley carried out with no great apparent rigour. At the Battle of Bosworth in August 1485, where Richard III ordered him to join his ranks on pain of seeing his son Lord Strange, held hostage, executed, he kept his troops back from the field, refusing to commit to either side until the critical moment when his brother William's intervention, and then his own, tipped the battle in favour of his stepson Henry Tudor. Richly rewarded by the new king, he became Earl of Derby and one of the great beneficiaries of the new regime, a belated crowning of a caution cultivated over more than thirty years.
