The Earl of Warwick's younger brother, John Neville at first fully shared his family's commitment to the Yorkist cause, fighting alongside his father and brother from the First Battle of St Albans in 1455. Captured at the Second Battle of St Albans in 1461, he was released after Edward IV's accession to the throne and went on to distinguish himself as one of the new regime's ablest military commanders.

It was in the north of England, where the last pockets of Lancastrian resistance remained, that he made his most brilliant mark: he won the decisive battles of Hedgeley Moor and Hexham in 1464, the latter resulting in the capture and execution of the Duke of Somerset. Edward IV rewarded him with the wealthy earldom of Northumberland, confiscated from the Percys, a distinction that for a time seemed to promise the junior Neville branch a lasting rise.

The reversal came in 1470, when the king, seeking to secure the Percys' loyalty to counterbalance the Nevilles' growing influence, restored the earldom to its traditional Lancastrian holder and offered Montagu, in compensation, only the lesser and, more importantly, less lucrative title of marquess. Feeling cheated after years of unfailing loyalty, he threw in his lot with his brother Warwick, then in open rebellion, and helped bring about Henry VI's brief restoration. He died at his side at the Battle of Barnet in April 1471, under circumstances chroniclers report inconsistently, some suggesting he tried to turn back to Edward IV at the very moment of battle, before being killed, perhaps by his own allies, who suspected him of treachery.
