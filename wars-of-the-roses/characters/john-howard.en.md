Descended through his mother from the old Mowbray family, Dukes of Norfolk, John Howard saw this rich inheritance slip from his grasp on the last duke's death in 1476, his heiress niece favoured by Edward IV over his own claim, though better founded in law. An experienced knight, he served the Yorkist crown faithfully throughout Edward IV's reign without ever gaining the recognition he felt he deserved.

The king's death in 1483 brought unhoped-for redress: Richard III, seeking to secure solid support among the nobility, finally granted him the dukedom of Norfolk he had coveted for seven years, at the same time setting aside the legitimate Mowbray heirs. This decisive favour sealed unwavering loyalty to the new king: Howard actively took part in crushing Buckingham's rebellion that same year and became one of the regime's military pillars.

At the Battle of Bosworth in August 1485, he commanded the vanguard of the royal army against the Earl of Oxford's troops, in the most exposed military role in Richard III's deployment. The night before the battle, according to a widely reported tradition, an anonymous note slipped into his tent warned him: "Jockey of Norfolk, be not too bold, for Dickon thy master is bought and sold," a hint at rumours of treachery within the royal ranks. Howard ignored it and died fighting alongside Richard III, one of the few great lords to remain loyal to the king to the very end.
