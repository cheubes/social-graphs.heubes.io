The daughter of René of Anjou, a king more in title than in fact, Margaret brought neither dowry nor decisive alliance to her 1445 marriage to Henry VI: the match, negotiated by William de la Pole, cost England the cession of Maine instead, which earned the young queen lasting unpopularity from the outset. At fifteen, transplanted to a country whose language she did not speak, she had to quickly learn to navigate a court divided into rival factions.

When Henry VI's madness struck in 1453, shortly after the birth of their only son Edward, Margaret understood before anyone else that the regency sought by Richard of York directly threatened her child's future. She proved a far more determined political operator than her husband: she rallied around her the great lords who had remained loyal to the crown, the Beauforts, the Percys, the Cliffords, and turned a court quarrel into a dynastic cause. After York's death at Wakefield in 1460, she won the Second Battle of St Albans but failed to retake London, leaving Edward IV free to have himself crowned.

Exiled to Scotland and then to France, she never gave up, and in 1470 negotiated an alliance as unexpected as it was improbable with her former enemy the Earl of Warwick, sealed by her son's marriage to his own daughter, Anne Neville. Henry VI's Readeption lasted only a few months: landing back in England too late, she watched her only son die at the Battle of Tewkesbury in 1471, the final collapse of the cause she had fought for over twenty years. Captured and imprisoned, she was eventually ransomed by King Louis XI of France in 1475 and died seven years later, in relative poverty, on her native Angevin lands.
