Edmund Beaufort's eldest son, Henry was only nineteen when he watched his father killed by York's supporters at St Albans in 1455. He inherited the ducal title along with a personal hostility toward the House of York that never wavered, sharpened further by wounds he himself received in that battle.

A leading Lancastrian commander, he fought at Blore Heath and Northampton in 1459-60 before sharing, with the rest of his side, the crushing defeat at Towton in 1461, the bloodiest battle of the entire war, from which he narrowly escaped capture by fleeing to Scotland. Over the following years he waged relentless guerrilla warfare in the north of England on the Lancastrian cause's behalf, besieging one fortress after another with mixed fortune.

In a turn rare enough for chroniclers to remark on, he submitted to Edward IV in 1462 and received a full pardon: the king even showered him with favour, installed him at court and included him in tournaments and hunting parties, hoping to win over so capable an opponent. The reconciliation lasted only a year: in 1463 Somerset defected once more to rejoin Henry VI, enraging Edward IV, who felt personally betrayed. Captured after the defeat at Hexham in May 1464, he was executed on the spot without trial, this second about-face costing him a punishment his first capture, at Towton, had not.
