The last surviving son of Edmund Beaufort, 2nd Duke, Edmund witnessed his father's death at St Albans as a child, and then, as a young man, his elder brother Henry's execution after Hexham in 1464. His own position was made all the more precarious by Parliament, by then dominated by Yorkists, having meanwhile confiscated the Beaufort family's titles and lands, leaving him Duke of Somerset in name only, recognised as such solely by loyalists of the Lancastrian cause.

Exiled in France and then Burgundy through the darkest years of the Lancastrian party, he did not return to England until 1471, on the strength of Margaret of Anjou and the young Prince Edward's landing, hoping to overthrow Edward IV while he was still occupied fighting Warwick's forces. Circumstances turned against him: news of Warwick's death at Barnet reached the Lancastrian troops as they were already marching north, depriving them of a decisive reinforcement.

Commanding the Lancastrian wing at the Battle of Tewkesbury on 4 May 1471, he attempted a flanking manoeuvre that dissolved into confusion and precipitated his side's rout. Taking refuge with several companions in the nearby abbey, he was dragged out despite the right of sanctuary on Edward IV's orders, given a summary trial before a military tribunal presided over by the Duke of Gloucester, and executed in the marketplace two days later. His death extinguished the legitimate line of the Dukes of Somerset and deprived the Lancastrian cause of its last great military leader.
