Orphaned by the age of four, Richard inherited the Dukedom of York in 1415 on the death of his uncle, killed at Agincourt, and, through his mother Anne Mortimer, a genealogical claim to the throne tracing back to Lionel of Antwerp, Edward III's second surviving son, older than the House of Lancaster's own claim through the fourth son. Having become one of the kingdom's greatest landowners, he served the crown loyally for two decades, as lieutenant in France and then as Lieutenant of Ireland.

Systematically shut out of the royal council by the Beauforts, whom he blamed for the military disasters in France, he returned from Ireland in 1450 to demand a place in the kingdom's government. Henry VI's madness, which struck in 1453, offered him an unhoped-for chance to finally wield power: named Protector of the Realm by an anxious Parliament, he governed competently until the king's recovery, who promptly dismissed him and restored Somerset to office.

Pushed to the limit, York took up arms in 1455 and killed Somerset at St Albans, though without then seeking to overthrow Henry VI himself. A second protectorate, in 1455-56, ended in the same humiliating dismissal. Only in 1460, after years of mounting tension and a temporary exile in Ireland, did he openly claim the throne before a Parliament stunned by the boldness of the move. A compromise, the Act of Accord, made him Henry VI's designated heir at the expense of the king's own son, enraging Queen Margaret of Anjou. He did not long enjoy this political victory: marching north to put down Lancastrian resistance, he was drawn out from the walls of his castle at Sandal and killed at the Battle of Wakefield on 30 December 1460. His head, crowned with a paper crown in mockery of his claims, was displayed on the walls of the city of York; his eldest son Edward inherited his cause and became king the following year.
