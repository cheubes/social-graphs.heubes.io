A companion-in-arms of Edward IV since the 1461 campaign, William Hastings fought at his side at Towton and then became his chamberlain, an office that placed him at the heart of the royal household and earned him a barony in the very year of the king's accession. Entrusted with diplomatic as well as military missions, he also represented English interests in negotiations with Burgundy, an alliance he helped strengthen.

His rivalry with the Woodville family, whose rise he considered self-serving and dangerous to the balance of the court, never stopped him remaining unfailingly loyal to the king himself: during Warwick's 1469-70 rebellion he stayed true to Edward IV, accompanied him into Burgundian exile, and largely organised the victorious return of 1471, fighting at Barnet where he commanded the left wing of the royal army. Edward IV rewarded this constant loyalty with considerable lands and offices, making him one of the most powerful men in the kingdom.

On Edward IV's death in 1483, he at first backed Richard of Gloucester against the Woodvilles, whose growing influence over the young Edward V had worried him for years. But once the protectorate was established, he firmly opposed Gloucester stripping the young king of the crown, remaining attached to the principle of dynastic legitimacy despite his hostility toward the dowager queen's family. Summoned to a council at the Tower of London on 13 June 1483, he was arrested there on Richard's orders, accused without evidence of conspiring with the Woodvilles and of sorcery, and executed on the spot, without trial, on a simple piece of timber in the fortress courtyard.
