John Clifford was only twenty when his father, Thomas Clifford, 8th Baron, was killed at the First Battle of St Albans in 1455, cut down by York's supporters while defending the approaches to the town alongside Edmund Beaufort and Northumberland. This loss, suffered in the war's very first armed clash, forged in the young baron an implacable hatred of the House of York that became, from then on, as much a personal motive as a political cause.

Lord of extensive estates in Yorkshire and Westmorland, he placed his lands and men at Queen Margaret of Anjou's service and became one of the most active commanders of Lancastrian resistance in the north. It was at the Battle of Wakefield in December 1460 that his revenge found its darkest expression: after the Yorkist rout and the death of Richard of York himself, Clifford pursued the young Edmund, Earl of Rutland, the duke's second son, as he tried to flee across the town bridge, and killed him personally despite his young age, seventeen, and his having played no part in commanding the battle.

This act, widely reported by chroniclers and later amplified by dramatic tradition, notably in Shakespeare, earned Clifford the nickname "the Butcher" that has stuck to his memory ever since. He did not long outlive his revenge, however: weeks later, on 28 March 1461, he was killed by an arrow in the skirmish at Ferrybridge, the outpost engagement before the Battle of Towton fought the following day, never living to see the outcome of the campaign he had helped reignite.
