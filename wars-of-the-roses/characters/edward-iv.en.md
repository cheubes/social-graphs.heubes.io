Richard of York's eldest son, Edward was only eighteen when his father's death at Wakefield in December 1460 made him, overnight, head of the Yorkist party. He avenged that defeat as early as February 1461 at Mortimer's Cross, then, after entering London and being proclaimed king by popular acclaim, crushed the Lancastrian army at Towton on 29 March, in a snowstorm, in the bloodiest battle ever fought on English soil. Tall, athletic, renowned for his charisma and his taste for pleasure as much as for his tactical sense, he consolidated his throne through successive campaigns in the north up to 1464.

His secret 1464 marriage to Elizabeth Woodville, a commoner widow met without his council's knowledge, deeply alienated his staunchest supporter, the Earl of Warwick, then engaged in negotiations for a French match. The meteoric rise of the large Woodville kinship, showered with titles and advantageous marriages, worsened the rift, which erupted into open rebellion in 1469-70: captured and then released by Warwick, Edward was eventually forced to flee to Burgundy in October 1470 when Warwick restored Henry VI to the throne.

Back in England in spring 1471 with a handful of followers and financial backing from his brother-in-law the Duke of Burgundy, he regained the throne in two decisive battles: Barnet, where Warwick was killed, and then Tewkesbury, where the young Lancastrian Prince of Wales perished, ending all organised resistance. Over the next twelve years he ruled unchallenged, restored royal finances, led a brief campaign in France concluded by an advantageous treaty, and governed with a firmness his first reign had lacked. His premature death in April 1483, probably from pneumonia or a stroke, left two underage sons whose tragic fate immediately reopened the succession crisis his own reign had seemingly closed.
