A young man of obscure Flemish origin, probably the son of a boatman from Tournai, Perkin Warbeck appeared on the political scene in 1491 at Cork, in Ireland, where his striking resemblance to Edward IV's sons led local merchants to press him into claiming to be none other than Richard of Shrewsbury, the younger of the "Princes in the Tower," said to have escaped the murder meant to kill him in 1483, only his elder brother having actually been killed by the assassins.

His bearing and manner, backed by the careful education he then received to fit his assumed role, were enough to convince several European courts hostile to Henry VII: Margaret of Burgundy, Edward IV's sister, publicly recognised him as her nephew and funded his ventures, while the courts of Scotland, where James IV had him marry a close relative, and of France received him for a time as a legitimate prince, a convenient diplomatic tool against England.

Backed by this funding and by foreign troops, he made several attempts to invade England and overthrow Henry VII, landing notably in Kent in 1495 and then in Cornwall in 1497, but never met the popular uprising he had hoped for: his English supporters remained too few to overturn an already firmly established regime. Captured in September 1497 after his last attempt collapsed, he was at first treated with relative clemency by Henry VII, curious to study up close the man who had threatened his throne, before being imprisoned in the Tower of London. Implicated in 1499 in an escape attempt plotted with Edward, Earl of Warwick, another prisoner of royal blood, he was executed at Tyburn, ending the last serious Yorkist claims to the English throne.
