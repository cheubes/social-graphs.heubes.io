A grandson of John of Gaunt through the legitimised Beaufort line, Edmund belonged to Henry VI's closest kin and, on that account, held a privileged place at court from his youth. He served first in France, where in 1448 he inherited the command left vacant by William de la Pole's disgrace, in an already badly deteriorated situation for English arms.

His handling of the Duchy of Normandy turned to disaster: unable to contain the renewed French offensive after the truce of Tours broke down, he surrendered Rouen in 1449 and lost the rest of the province within a matter of months, a collapse as swift as it was humiliating that ruined his military reputation. Back in England, he nonetheless became Henry VI's most influential favourite, accumulating offices and royal favour to the fury of Richard of York, who saw in him the heir to every fault previously laid at Suffolk's door.

The rivalry between the two men, fed by York's systematic exclusion from the royal council, boiled over in 1455 when York marched on London at the head of an army to demand Somerset's dismissal. The standoff turned into armed confrontation in the streets of St Albans on 22 May: the royal guard, ill-prepared, was quickly overwhelmed, and Somerset himself was killed in the melee while trying to take shelter in an inn. This battle, the first of the Wars of the Roses, made his death the symbol of the conflict's irreversible turn from political rivalry into open civil war.
