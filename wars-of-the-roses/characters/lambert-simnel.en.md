The son of an Oxford tradesman according to the most widely accepted account, Lambert Simnel was still a child when a priest named Richard Simon noticed his striking resemblance to Edward IV's sons and set about training him to pass as a prince of royal blood. The initial plan, to present him as one of the missing Princes in the Tower, was dropped in favour of a claim judged more credible: that of Edward, Earl of Warwick, nephew of both Edward IV and Richard III, in reality held very much alive as Henry VII's prisoner in the Tower of London.

Presented to Yorkist supporters in Ireland in 1487, where English royal authority remained weak and hostility to the Tudors still strong, he was welcomed with immediate enthusiasm by the great Irish lords and crowned in Dublin as "Edward VI," carried in triumph through the city's streets. The Earl of Lincoln and Margaret of York, Duchess of Burgundy, seeing in this imposture a chance to revive the Yorkist cause, funded an army of Irish and German mercenaries that landed with him in Wales.

The venture ended at the Battle of Stoke Field on 16 June 1487, where Simnel's motley army was crushed by Henry VII's troops, who captured the boy without difficulty. Aware that he was merely a tool in the hands of adults far more culpable than himself, the king showed unexpected clemency: rather than execute him, he assigned him to the royal kitchens as a spit-turner, from where Simnel later rose through the ranks to become a royal falconer, an improbable second act that let him, unlike most actors in this story, die of natural causes decades later.
