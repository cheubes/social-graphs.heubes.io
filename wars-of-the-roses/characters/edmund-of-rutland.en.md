The second surviving son of Richard of York and Cecily Neville, Edmund was born on 17 May 1443 in Rouen, where his father was then serving as the king's lieutenant-general in France. Raised alongside his elder brother Edward, from whom he was separated by only eleven months, he received with him, in 1454, the office of royal commissioner in Herefordshire, a largely honorary responsibility given his young age.

In 1459, when Lancastrian troops sacked the family residence at Ludlow, sixteen-year-old Edmund found himself, with his mother and young brother Richard, directly exposed to the victors' wrath, forced like them to appear as a suppliant before the triumphant lords. Few sources dwell on his own personality, most chronicles mentioning him only in connection with his father's and elder brother's affairs.

In December 1460, he accompanied Richard of York and their uncle the Earl of Salisbury on the northern campaign meant to put down Lancastrian resistance, and was at his side at Sandal Castle when the Yorkist forces, having rashly sortied from their fortifications, were crushed at the Battle of Wakefield on 30 December. Trying to flee the field across the town bridge, he was caught by John Clifford, whose own father had died at St Albans five years earlier, and killed personally despite his youth and his having played no part in commanding the battle. His head, like his father's, was displayed on the walls of the city of York; his death, at seventeen, became one of the most widely retold symbols of the conflict's cruelty.
