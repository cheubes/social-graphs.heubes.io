The son of the 12th Earl of Oxford, John de Vere was only twenty when his father and elder brother were executed in 1462 on Edward IV's orders, accused of plotting to restore Henry VI. Allowed to inherit the title despite this family disgrace, he concealed his still-intact Lancastrian loyalty for several years beneath an outward submission to the new regime.

He openly joined the Lancastrian cause when the Earl of Warwick switched sides in 1470 and commanded a wing of the army that briefly restored Henry VI to the throne. At the Battle of Barnet in April 1471, a tragic mix-up between his personal badge, a radiant star, and Edward IV's radiant sun sowed panic in the Lancastrian ranks, who mistook each other for the enemy in the fog, precipitating their rout. Oxford escaped capture and continued the resistance alone, seizing St Michael's Mount in Cornwall in 1473 and holding it for several months against royal troops before being forced to surrender.

Imprisoned for over ten years at Hammes Castle near Calais, he managed to escape in 1484 and immediately joined Henry Tudor in exile, putting at his service a military experience unmatched in his camp. He commanded the vanguard of Henry's army at the Battle of Bosworth in August 1485, where his tactics against the Duke of Norfolk's troops contributed decisively to victory. Showered with honours under the new regime, he became one of Henry VII's closest military advisers and died in 1513, after more than half a century spent, almost without interruption, in the service of the Lancastrian and then Tudor cause.
