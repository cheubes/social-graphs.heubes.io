Descended from an old Welsh family that had lost much of its influence after Owain Glyndŵr's failed revolt earlier in the century, Owen Tudor entered the royal household, where tradition holds he occupied a modest post, perhaps as squire or keeper of the wardrobe, in the circle of the young dowager queen Catherine of Valois, widowed by Henry V since 1422.

Around 1428, the two young people married in secret, a match as improbable as it was scandalous between a dowager queen and a mere untitled Welsh gentleman: the marriage, contracted in violation of a recent law forbidding dowager queens to remarry without the king and council's consent, only came to light afterwards and curiously drew little retaliation, perhaps thanks to the young Henry VI's quiet protectiveness toward his mother. Their union produced several children, among them Edmund and Jasper, the king's half-brothers and the future founders of the Tudor house's political fortune.

Remaining loyal to the Lancastrian cause after Catherine's death in 1437 and the civil war's outbreak, he fought alongside his son Jasper and raised troops in Wales in support of Henry VI. Captured after the Lancastrian defeat at Mortimer's Cross in February 1461, he was taken to Hereford and executed on the orders of the young Edward, then Earl of March and future Edward IV, who marked his victory with this show of force. Tradition, as reported by chroniclers, holds that a woman, seized by madness or pity, washed his face and combed his hair after his beheading, one last act of humanity for a man whose grandson Henry would become King of England a quarter-century later.
