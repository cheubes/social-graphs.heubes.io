Queen Elizabeth Woodville's elder brother, Anthony owed his rapid rise at Edward IV's court to that family tie, following his sister's secret 1464 marriage. A knight renowned for his bravery as much as his piety, he distinguished himself in 1467 in a celebrated tournament at Smithfield against the Bastard of Burgundy, then accompanied the king into Burgundian exile in 1470-71 and fought at his side at Barnet and Tewkesbury.

A passionate man of letters, keen on translation and chivalry, he had William Caxton print his own translation of the "Dictes and Sayings of the Philosophers" in 1477, the first dated book printed in England, which he presented in person to the king and his sister the queen in a ceremony that has become well known. Appointed governor of the young Prince of Wales, the future Edward V, he settled with him at Ludlow and oversaw his education for over a decade, instilling in him a taste for letters and chivalric exercise.

On Edward IV's death in April 1483, he was escorting the young king to London for his coronation when their party was intercepted at Northampton by Richard of Gloucester and the Duke of Buckingham. Arrested on the spot along with several other relatives of the dowager queen, he was taken to Pontefract Castle where, without a formal trial before his peers, he was executed on 25 June 1483, the day before Richard of Gloucester had himself proclaimed king. Shortly before his death, he composed a final poem steeped in Christian resignation, a rare surviving voice among the victims of those years of political purge.
