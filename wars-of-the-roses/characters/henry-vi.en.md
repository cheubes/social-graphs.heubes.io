Crowned king at nine months old on the death of his father Henry V in 1422, and King of France the following year on the death of his grandfather Charles VI under the Treaty of Troyes, Henry VI inherited a double crown that England could no longer afford to hold. The Hundred Years' War turned to disaster: Joan of Arc lifted the siege of Orléans as early as 1429, and the kingdom of France had almost entirely slipped from English control by the time Henry VI came of age.

Pious to a fault, timid, and little inclined to warfare or the practical exercise of power, he preferred founding colleges, at Eton and Cambridge, to running the kingdom, which he left to favourites such as William de la Pole and then Edmund Beaufort. His 1445 marriage to Margaret of Anjou, negotiated at the secret cost of ceding French territory, further discredited him with part of the nobility. From 1453, a first bout of mental stupor, from which he only intermittently recovered, left him incapable of governing and opened the way to the ambitions of Richard of York, whom Parliament made Protector of the Realm.

The rivalry between York and the court favourites degenerated into open civil war by 1455. Henry VI, captured at the First Battle of St Albans and again at Northampton in 1460, became the passive stake of a conflict his wife waged in his name. Deposed by Edward IV in 1461, briefly restored in 1470 during the "Readeption" orchestrated by the Earl of Warwick, he was recaptured and confined to the Tower of London after the Lancastrian defeat at Tewkesbury in 1471. He died there on the night of 21-22 May that year, very probably murdered on the orders of Edward IV, eager to extinguish any rival claim.
