Born in 1457 at Pembroke Castle months after his father Edmund Tudor's death, Henry descended from Edward III through a very distant and originally illegitimate line, the Beauforts, legitimised but explicitly barred from the royal succession by a clause of Henry IV's. He had little serious claim to the throne until the gradual disappearance of most direct Lancastrian heirs, Henry VI and his son Edward both dead by 1471, made him, almost by default, his party's last hope.

Exiled to Brittany at fourteen under his uncle Jasper's protection after the final defeat at Tewkesbury, he spent nearly fourteen years there, escaping several capture attempts orchestrated in turn by Edward IV and then Richard III, both of whom negotiated with the Duke of Brittany for his extradition. It was during this exile that he publicly pledged, at Christmas 1483, to marry Elizabeth of York should his cause triumph, uniting in a single oath both Lancastrian hopes and Yorkist opposition to Richard III.

Landing in Wales in August 1485 with a composite army of French mercenaries and English exiles assembled with the French king's backing, he marched inland gathering support as he went, never certain of his venture's outcome. He owed his decisive victory at Bosworth to the last-minute defection of Lord Stanley's troops, his stepfather, which tipped the course of the fighting at the critical moment and caused Richard III's death. Crowned on the very battlefield, he married Elizabeth of York the following year, symbolically reuniting the two rival houses, and founded a dynasty that ruled England for more than a century. His reign, marked by careful management of royal finances and the suppression of several late Yorkist rebellions, including those of Lambert Simnel and Perkin Warbeck, ended with his death in 1509.
