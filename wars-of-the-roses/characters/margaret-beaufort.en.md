A great-granddaughter of John of Gaunt through the legitimised Beaufort line, Margaret became, on her father's death when she was just one, one of the wealthiest heiresses in the kingdom, which made her hand a matter of dispute from her earliest years. Married at twelve to Edmund Tudor, she gave birth the following year to her only child, Henry, shortly after her husband's death in captivity, a delivery so perilous given her young age that it permanently damaged her health and left her unable to have further children by her later marriages.

Twice remarried, first to Henry Stafford, second son of the Duke of Buckingham, and then, widowed again, to Thomas Stanley around 1472, she devoted her entire political life to preserving and advancing the rights of her only son, exiled in Brittany since 1471. A learned and pious woman, she kept up a discreet but constant correspondence with her son and his circle, patiently weaving a network of loyalists within Yorkist England itself.

Secretly allied with Elizabeth Woodville after Richard III's usurpation in 1483, she was directly involved in Buckingham's failed rebellion that same year, which earned her placement under the supervised guardianship of her own husband Thomas Stanley, a lenient punishment that nonetheless let her continue orchestrating her son's return from within England. After the victory at Bosworth in 1485, now styled "My Lady the King's Mother," she wielded considerable influence at court, to the point of signing her letters with her title alone, with no mention of subjection to anyone but God. She spent her final years in intense religious devotion and academic patronage, founding chairs and colleges at Cambridge, notably Christ's College and St John's College, before dying in 1509, only weeks after her son.
