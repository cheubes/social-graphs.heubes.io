Head of the powerful Percy family, which had dominated north-east England for generations from its castles at Alnwick and Warkworth, Henry Percy inherited in 1455 an earldom already locked in simmering conflict with its Neville neighbours. This rivalry, born of disputes over land and precedence along the Scottish border, grew so bitter that the two families clashed in arms as early as 1453, at Heworth Moor, two years before the Wars of the Roses officially began.

The Nevilles' alliance with Richard of York naturally pushed the Percys into the opposing camp, that of the court and Queen Margaret of Anjou, turning a local feud into one of the war's harshest regional fault lines. Northumberland fought at the First Battle of St Albans in 1455, where his own father was killed, and distinguished himself at the Second Battle of St Albans in 1461, one of the few Lancastrian victories of the period, in which royal forces briefly recaptured Henry VI from Yorkist hands.

But the triumph was short-lived: weeks later, at Towton, the largest and bloodiest battle ever fought on English soil, the Lancastrian forces were crushed in the snow and mud by Edward IV's army. Northumberland was killed there alongside many other great Lancastrian lords, leaving the north of England, for a time, without a leader capable of rallying opposition to the new Yorkist dynasty.
