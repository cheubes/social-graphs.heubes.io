The third of Richard of York's sons to survive childhood, George was born in Dublin in 1449 while his father was serving as Lieutenant of Ireland. Made Duke of Clarence as soon as his elder brother Edward IV took the throne in 1461, he was for a time named heir presumptive while the king remained childless, a position that fed expectations events would never satisfy.

Edward IV's secret marriage to Elizabeth Woodville in 1464, and the subsequent rise of the new queen's family, alienated Clarence as much as it did the Earl of Warwick, whose eldest daughter Isabel he married in 1469 against the king's explicit prohibition. That union sealed his alignment with Warwick's rebellion: the two men briefly captured Edward IV, had the queen's father executed, and Clarence himself was for a time floated as a possible substitute king. The venture collapsed for lack of sufficient popular support, and the two rebels had to flee to France in 1470.

It was there that Warwick made his most spectacular about-face, allying with Margaret of Anjou to restore Henry VI, a scheme in which Clarence, his own claim to the throne now eclipsed by that of the Lancastrian house, saw no further personal advantage. He secretly negotiated his reconciliation with Edward IV and defected at the decisive moment of his brother's return to England in 1471, helping secure the victory at Barnet where Warwick was killed. Pardoned but never fully restored to royal trust, Clarence went on to provoke his brother repeatedly, even having a servant of his late wife executed without authorisation on a charge of poisoning her. Arrested on his own brother's orders in 1477, tried for treason before Parliament, he was executed in the Tower of London on 18 February 1478; legend holds, without solid evidence, that he was drowned in a butt of Malmsey wine.
