The eldest daughter of Edward IV and Elizabeth Woodville, born in 1466 at Westminster Abbey where her mother had taken refuge as a precaution, Elizabeth of York grew up at court as the first of the royal children, promised for a time by treaty to a son of the King of France before that planned match was unilaterally broken off in 1482, a snub that deeply humiliated her father.

Her position was overturned overnight on Edward IV's death in 1483: her uncle Richard of Gloucester seized the throne by having her parents' marriage declared illegitimate, stripping her and her siblings of all princely rank. Taking refuge in sanctuary with her mother while her two young brothers vanished in the Tower of London, she became, without seeking it, purely by the force of her birth, the symbol around which opposition to Richard III gathered, to the point that contemporary rumours even spoke of a planned marriage between uncle and niece after Queen Anne Neville's death in 1485, a rumour Richard III publicly denied.

Her mother and Margaret Beaufort secretly negotiated her marriage to Henry Tudor, the exiled Lancastrian claimant, who publicly pledged as early as Christmas 1483 to marry her should he win the crown, uniting in a single oath the hopes of both sides. The pledge was fulfilled after the victory at Bosworth in August 1485: their union, celebrated on 18 January 1486, symbolically reunited the Houses of York and Lancaster and founded the Tudor dynasty. An unassuming queen but visibly loved by her husband, she bore seven children, four of whom reached adulthood, among them the future Henry VIII, before dying in 1503 from complications of childbirth, on her thirty-seventh birthday.
