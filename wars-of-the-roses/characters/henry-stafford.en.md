Descended from Edward III through two separate lines, which made him one of the few peers of the realm able to claim blood as close to the royal family as the Yorks or Lancastrians themselves, Henry Stafford inherited the dukedom at five, his father killed defending Henry VI at the First Battle of St Albans. Married as a child to Catherine Woodville, the queen's sister, he grew up kept away from power under Edward IV, who openly distrusted his dynastic closeness to the crown and entrusted him with no responsibility matching his rank.

He saw the king's death in April 1483 as an unhoped-for chance to step out of the shadows: he allied at once with Richard of Gloucester, joining him as soon as he entered London, and actively helped him sideline the Woodvilles from power, notably in Anthony Woodville's arrest at Northampton. Showered with titles and considerable Welsh lands as reward, he became one of the chief architects of Richard III's usurpation, taking a direct part in William Hastings's arrest and in the propaganda campaign that legitimised the new king's accession.

The favour did not last: by autumn 1483, for reasons historians have never fully explained, perhaps personal disappointment, perhaps the influence of Margaret Beaufort, whose improvised gaoler he had become, Buckingham abruptly turned against Richard III and raised a Welsh rebellion in favour of the Lancastrian claimant Henry Tudor. Torrential rain made the Severn impassable and prevented the planned link-up with other centres of revolt; abandoned by his own troops, who deserted en masse, he tried to flee in disguise but was betrayed by a servant and captured. Brought before Richard III at Salisbury, he was executed without trial in the marketplace on 2 November 1483, without even obtaining the audience with the king he had requested.
