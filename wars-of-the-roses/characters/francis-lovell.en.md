A royal ward after his father's premature death, Francis Lovell was raised in the Earl of Warwick's household alongside other young nobles, where he formed a lasting friendship with Richard of Gloucester, two years his senior. This childhood closeness turned into absolute political loyalty once Richard became duke and then king: Lovell followed him through every office and became, on Richard III's accession in 1483, royal chamberlain and viscount, one of the innermost circle of the new regime.

His closeness to the king earned him, alongside William Catesby and Richard Ratcliffe, a place in a satirical pamphlet anonymously posted in London in 1484: "The Cat, the Rat, and Lovell our dog rule all England under a hog," a jibe at once at their names and at Richard III's heraldic badge, the white boar. The pamphlet's author, William Collingbourne, was executed for the act, proof of the regime's sensitivity to any visible mockery.

Fighting at Bosworth alongside Richard III in August 1485, he escaped the rout and took refuge for a time in sanctuary before continuing Yorkist resistance from exile. In 1487 he joined the Lambert Simnel rebellion alongside John de la Pole, Earl of Lincoln, landing with an army of Irish and German mercenaries in hopes of overthrowing Henry VII. After the defeat at Stoke Field on 16 June 1487, where Lincoln perished, Lovell vanished without any certain trace; a persistent legend, partly borne out by the eighteenth-century discovery of a skeleton in a secret chamber at his manor of Minster Lovell, holds that he starved to death there, hidden by a servant who was never able to return and free him.
