Born on 31 March 1519 at the château de Saint-Germain-en-Laye, the second son of Francis I and Claude of France, he receives at birth the title of Duke of Orléans and the first name of his godfather, Henry VIII of England. Held hostage in Spain with his elder brother Francis from March 1526 to July 1530 under the Treaty of Madrid, he carries lasting psychological scars from the ordeal, becoming hypochondriac and having a difficult relationship with his father, who favours his younger brother Charles.

Becoming dauphin on his brother Francis's death in 1536, he marries Catherine de' Medici on 28 October 1533, but his heart belongs to Diane de Poitiers, his confidante since the age of fifteen. He becomes King of France at twenty-eight, on the death of Francis I in 1547, and centralises power, creating the présidiaux courts in 1551 and relying on four secretaries of state. He continued the Italian Wars against the Habsburgs, occupying the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Verdun and Toul, despite defeat at Saint-Quentin in 1557, until the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis of 3 April 1559, which ended the conflict and sealed the peace through the marriage of his daughter Elisabeth to Philip II of Spain, at the cost of returning Piedmont and Savoy. A devout Catholic influenced by Diane de Poitiers, he also worked to suppress the spread of Protestantism in the kingdom, creating the Chambre Ardente as early as 1547 and then hardening repression through the Edicts of Châteaubriant in 1551 and Compiègne in 1557.

He died ten days after being struck in the eye by a lance splinter during a tournament on rue Saint-Antoine held to celebrate the peace, Gabriel de Montgomery, captain of his Scottish Guard, unable to pull his blow in time; carried to the Hôtel des Tournelles, he lingers in agony despite the care of surgeon Ambroise Paré and physician Andreas Vesalius, and dies on 10 July 1559. A patron of the arts, he employs the architect Philibert de l'Orme and supports the Pléiade poets Ronsard and du Bellay.
