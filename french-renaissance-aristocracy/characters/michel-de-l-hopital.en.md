Born between 1503 and 1507 in Chaptuzat, in the Puy-de-Dôme, the son of Jean de l'Hôpital, physician to the Duke of Bourbon, he studies from 1523 to 1534 at the universities of Padua and Bologna, becoming a professor of civil law and earning a reputation as a jurist. A parliamentary councillor from 1537, after his marriage to Marie Morin, he becomes superintendent of finances in 1554.

A trained jurist, he distinguished himself in diplomatic missions before being appointed Chancellor of France on 30 June 1560 under Francis II, a post he retains under Charles IX and Catherine de' Medici. There he pursued a policy of religious tolerance toward Protestants, arguing from the Estates-General of Orléans onward for appeasement rather than repression, secures the holding of the Colloquy of Poissy in 1561, and secured the promulgation of the Edict of January 1562, granting Protestants a limited right of worship, in an attempt to prevent civil war; he also has the Ordinance of Moulins adopted in 1566, strengthening royal authority and affirming the inalienability of the crown's domain.

This policy of conciliation, which hardline Catholics blamed for triggering the first War of Religion, earned him the open hostility of the Cardinal of Lorraine. After refusing in 1568 to seal a papal bull calling for the extermination of heretics, he loses the seals and is forced to resign on 6 February 1573. Pushed from power, he retired to his estate, where he died on 13 March 1573 in Boutigny-sur-Essonne, and is buried in the church of Champmotteux.
