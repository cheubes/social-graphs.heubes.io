A trained jurist, he distinguished himself in diplomatic missions before being appointed Chancellor of France by Catherine de' Medici in 1560. There he pursued a policy of religious tolerance toward Protestants, arguing from the Estates-General of Orléans onward for appeasement rather than repression, and secured the promulgation of the Edict of January 1562, granting Protestants a limited right of worship, in an attempt to prevent civil war. This policy of conciliation, which hardline Catholics blamed for triggering the first War of Religion, earned him the open hostility of the Cardinal of Lorraine, who engineered his fall from favour in 1568. Pushed from power, he retired to his estate, where he died in 1573.
