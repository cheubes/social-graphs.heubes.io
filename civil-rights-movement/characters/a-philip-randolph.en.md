Born Asa Philip Randolph on 15 April 1889 in Crescent City, Florida, the son of an African Methodist Episcopal Church pastor, he moves with his family to Jacksonville in 1891 and then to Harlem in 1911, where he abandons a hoped-for acting career to study political science and economics at the City College of New York. He co-founds the radical, socialist-leaning magazine The Messenger in 1917.

Founder and leader of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, organized in 1925 at the Pullman Company, which employed mostly African American workers, the first major Black labor union in the United States, he secures an agreement after years of struggle in 1937, then as early as 1941 gets President Roosevelt to ban racial discrimination in the defense industry, by threatening to organize a march on Washington.

A respected elder statesman of the movement in the 1960s, he was the original initiator of the 1963 March on Washington, entrusting its logistical organization to his protégé Bayard Rustin, and delivered one of the opening speeches at the gathering before the Lincoln Memorial. Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964, he dies on 16 May 1979 in Manhattan, aged ninety.
