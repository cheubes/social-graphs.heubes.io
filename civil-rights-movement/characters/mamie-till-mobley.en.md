Born Mamie Elizabeth Carthan on 23 November 1921 in Webb, Mississippi, she migrates north as a child during the Great Migration, settling with her family in Argo, Illinois, by 1924; a brilliant student, she is the first African American on her high school's honor roll. She marries Louis Till at eighteen, in 1940, and their son Emmett is born nine months later.

After her son's murder in 1955, she insisted that his mutilated body be displayed in an open casket at his Chicago funeral, so the whole country could see, in her words, "what they did to my baby." Tens of thousands come to view his remains, and photographs published by the Black press spark national outrage that turns the crime into a symbol of racial injustice.

Earning her teaching degree in 1960, she spends twenty-three years teaching in Chicago and founds the Emmett Till Players, a youth group trained in public speaking and civic engagement; she leads one of the most successful fundraising speaking tours in the NAACP's history. Earning a master's degree in 1976, she publishes her memoir, Death of Innocence, in 2003, the year she dies of heart failure, aged eighty-one; her tombstone reads: "Her pain united a nation."
