Born on 16 December 1938 in Marion, Alabama, a farmer and woodcutter living in poverty with his sister, mother and grandfather in a house with no running water, he is an Army veteran and a deacon, the youngest in the history of Marion's St. James Baptist Church, where he takes part in the voter registration drive.

In 1962, after watching his grandfather Cager Lee and his mother Viola Jackson turned away from the county courthouse where they tried to register, he joins the civil rights movement and writes to a federal judge to protest this treatment, symptomatic of the broader denial of rights faced by Black Southerners under Jim Crow. He took part on 18 February 1965 in a night march in Marion held in support of a jailed activist. State troopers violently charged the demonstrators; trying to protect his mother and grandfather, Jackson, unarmed, was beaten and then shot by a trooper inside a nearby café.

He died of his wounds eight days later. His death pushed James Bevel and the SCLC to conceive the Selma to Montgomery march, originally meant to carry his coffin to Governor Wallace's doorstep; the idea grew into the historic march that helped speed passage of the 1965 Voting Rights Act.
