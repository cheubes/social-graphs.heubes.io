Born on 12 January 1920 in Marshall, Texas, the son of a theology professor at Wiley College, he enters that college at fourteen after skipping several grades, then earns a Bachelor of Divinity from Howard University in 1941, where Professor Howard Thurman introduces him to Gandhian civil disobedience.

Co-founder in 1942 of the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE), one of the movement's oldest organizations devoted to nonviolent civil disobedience, he became its national director in 1961. Under his leadership CORE organized the Freedom Rides that year, interracial bus journeys meant to test enforcement of a Supreme Court ruling banning segregation in interstate travel; arrested in Jackson after rejoining the campaign's final leg, he serves forty days at Parchman Penitentiary.

The buses were attacked and burned in Alabama, but the campaign, later taken up by the SNCC, forced the federal government to enforce desegregation. He is counted among the "Big Six" who organized the 1963 March on Washington, but is the only one absent from it: arrested that August in Plaquemine, Louisiana, during protests against police brutality, he refuses to post bail and, facing death threats, has to be smuggled out in the back of a hearse. He dies on 9 July 1999 in Fredericksburg, Virginia, aged seventy-nine.
