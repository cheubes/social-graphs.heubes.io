Born on 2 July 1925 in Decatur, Mississippi, he serves in the US Army during the Second World War before earning a degree from Alcorn State University.

He became the NAACP's first field secretary for Mississippi in 1954, one of the most dangerous states in the South for a Black activist, championing voting rights, school desegregation and the integration of public facilities. He investigated Emmett Till's murder in 1955 and supported James Meredith's admission to the University of Mississippi in 1962.

He was shot in the back outside his Jackson home on 12 June 1963, hours after President Kennedy's televised address on civil rights, by Byron De La Beckwith, a white supremacist and Citizens' Council member. Despite physical evidence linking him to the crime, two all-white juries fail to convict him in 1964; it takes until 1994, and a mixed jury, for him to finally be found guilty. Evers is posthumously awarded the Spingarn Medal, and his wife Myrlie Evers-Williams becomes a prominent activist in her own right.
