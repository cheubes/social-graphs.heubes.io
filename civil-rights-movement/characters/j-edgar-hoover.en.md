Born John Edgar Hoover on 1 January 1895 in Washington, D.C., he earns his law degree from George Washington University in 1917 and joins the Department of Justice that same year, initially working under Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer during anti-communist operations.

Appointed director of the Bureau of Investigation on 10 May 1924, at twenty-nine, he modernizes investigative methods through fingerprinting and forensic analysis; once the agency becomes the FBI in 1935, its authority expands considerably, and he remains its director under six presidents until his death. He viewed the civil rights movement, and Martin Luther King in particular, as infiltrated by communists and a threat to the American social order. He authorized the wiretapping of King and fueled a harassment campaign aimed at discrediting him.

The domestic counterintelligence program COINTELPRO (1956-1971), which he expanded in the mid-1960s, also targeted the Nation of Islam and the SNCC, part of a surveillance and disruption strategy that was only fully revealed after his death on 2 May 1972 in Washington; the Church Committee (1975-1976) later documents the scale of this illegal surveillance, targeting eight presidents, opposition groups and the movement's leaders.
