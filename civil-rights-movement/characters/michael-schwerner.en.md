Born Michael Henry Schwerner on 6 November 1939 in Pelham, New York, into a Jewish family, he attends Pelham Memorial High School and first studies veterinary medicine at Michigan State University, before switching to sociology at Cornell University and then joining Columbia University's School of Social Work; he is a childhood friend of Robert Reich, the future US Secretary of Labor.

A New York social worker, he moved with his wife Rita to Mississippi in January 1964 to open a CORE field office in Meridian, where he organized Black voter registration and trained volunteers ahead of Freedom Summer. His visible activism made him a particular target of the local Ku Klux Klan.

On 21 June 1964, while investigating a church burning with James Chaney and Andrew Goodman, the three men were arrested and then handed over at night to Klan members who killed them; he dies at twenty-four. The ensuing federal investigation, popularized under the code name "Mississippi Burning," led to the first federal convictions of white supremacists for a crime of this kind. He is posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2014.
