Born on 4 February 1913 in Tuskegee, Alabama, she grows up amid the daily reality of Jim Crow segregation, separate water fountains and transit, and joins the NAACP in 1943, investigating the assault on Recy Taylor in 1944 among other cases. Trained in civil disobedience at the Highlander Folk School, she becomes secretary of the Montgomery NAACP branch.

She refused on 1 December 1955 to give up her seat to a white passenger on a Montgomery bus, a deliberate act rather than simple fatigue, as the popular myth has it: "I was not particularly tired physically, but if there was anything I was tired of, it was giving in," she later explains.

Her arrest triggered a bus boycott led by Martin Luther King that lasted 381 days and ended on 13 November 1956 with a Supreme Court ruling declaring segregation on public transit unconstitutional. Relocating to Detroit in 1957, she works for congressman John Conyers from 1965 until her retirement in 1988, and founds the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute in 1987 to teach young people civil rights history. Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1996 and the Congressional Gold Medal in 1999, she dies on 24 October 2005 and becomes the first woman to lie in state at the Capitol.
