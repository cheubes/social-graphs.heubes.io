A theology student, he took part in the Nashville sit-ins and then became one of the original thirteen Freedom Riders in 1961, repeatedly beaten in the process. Elected SNCC chairman in 1963, he was the youngest speaker at the March on Washington, where his speech, deemed too radical, was toned down at the request of more moderate organizers.

On March 7, 1965, his skull was fractured by state troopers at the head of the Selma march, on "Bloody Sunday," which pushed President Johnson to speed passage of the Voting Rights Act. Voted out of the SNCC chairmanship in 1966 by Black Power supporters, he continued his activism as a Democratic congressman for more than three decades.
