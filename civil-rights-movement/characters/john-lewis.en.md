Born on 21 February 1940 in Troy, Alabama, to sharecropper parents, he studies theology at American Baptist College and then Fisk University and becomes an ordained Baptist minister. He takes part in the Nashville sit-ins and then becomes one of the original thirteen Freedom Riders in 1961, repeatedly beaten in the process.

Elected SNCC chairman in 1963, he was the youngest speaker at the March on Washington, where his speech, deemed too radical, was toned down at the request of more moderate organizers. On 7 March 1965, his skull was fractured by state troopers at the head of the Selma march, on "Bloody Sunday," which pushed President Johnson to speed passage of the Voting Rights Act.

Voted out of the SNCC chairmanship in 1966 by Black Power supporters, he leads the Voter Education Project from 1971 to 1977, then begins a political career: elected to Atlanta's city council in 1981, he becomes a Democratic congressman for Georgia in 1986, a post he holds until his death, from advanced pancreatic cancer, on 17 July 2020, aged eighty.
