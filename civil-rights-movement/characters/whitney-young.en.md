Born on 31 July 1921 in Lincoln Ridge, Kentucky, the son of the director of the Lincoln Institute, a school for Black youth, and a mother who became the first African American to head a post office in the state, he earns a degree from Kentucky State University and studies electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology during the Second World War, before earning a master's in social work from the University of Minnesota in 1947 and joining the National Urban League's St. Paul chapter.

He became executive director of the National Urban League in 1961, at forty, and steered it toward a more activist role, growing its staff from thirty-eight to sixteen hundred employees and its annual budget from $325,000 to more than $6.1 million within four years, building ties with major corporations and the federal government, a strategy some criticized but that proved effective, to secure the hiring and training of African Americans; he also launches programs such as Street Academy and New Thrust.

Counted among the "Big Six" who organized the 1963 March on Washington, he embodied a more institutional, negotiated path than civil disobedience, without ever breaking from the movement's other leaders, and advises Presidents Kennedy, Johnson and Nixon. Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1969, he drowned accidentally in a swimming pool on 11 March 1971 while attending a conference in Lagos, Nigeria, aged forty-nine.
