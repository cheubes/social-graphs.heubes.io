Born on 30 August 1901 in St. Louis, Missouri, he loses his mother to tuberculosis at four and is adopted by relatives in St. Paul, Minnesota, where he experiences less segregation than in the Deep South. He studies sociology and economics at the University of Minnesota, becoming the first African American editor of the student newspaper, before joining the Kansas City Call as an editor in 1923 and the local NAACP chapter.

Recruited in 1931 by Walter White as an assistant at the NAACP's national office, he edits The Crisis magazine from 1934 to 1949 and helps defend the Scottsboro Boys. A journalist and then a NAACP administrator, he became its executive secretary on 11 April 1955 and led the organization for more than two decades, backing the Brown v. Board of Education ruling and Rosa Parks's bus boycott, while favoring institutional lobbying and litigation over mass direct action.

Wary of the more radical methods championed by the SNCC and the Nation of Islam, he nonetheless helped organize the 1963 March on Washington alongside the movement's other major figures, while keeping his distance from the Black Power turn that followed. Married to Aminda "Minnie" Badeau in 1929, with no children, he leaves the NAACP's leadership in 1977 and dies on 8 September 1981 in New York, aged eighty.
