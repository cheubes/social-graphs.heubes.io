Born on 25 August 1919 in Clio, Alabama, he earns a law degree from the University of Alabama and serves in the Army Air Forces during the Second World War before entering politics. Initially a moderate on racial issues, he takes a hard turn after losing the 1958 gubernatorial primary, vowing never again to be "out-segged," and adopts uncompromising segregationism.

Elected Governor of Alabama in 1962, he promised in his inaugural address "segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever." On 11 June 1963, he symbolically stood in the doorway of the University of Alabama in a failed attempt to block the enrollment of two Black students; his administration is also criticized after the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing in Birmingham that same year, which kills four young Black girls.

A national figure of segregationism, he ran for president several times on a platform opposing integration, winning nearly ten million votes (13.53%) and forty-six electoral votes as a third-party candidate in 1968. Gravely wounded by five gunshots during his 1972 presidential campaign, he is left paralyzed, and declares himself repentant during his final term as governor (1983-1987), seeking forgiveness from the Black leaders he had fought. He dies on 13 September 1998 from complications of Parkinson's disease and septicemia.
