Born on 29 June 1941 in Port of Spain, Trinidad, he emigrates to New York at eleven, in 1952, and studies philosophy at Howard University, where he joins the SNCC in his first year and takes part in the Freedom Rides.

He takes part in Freedom Summer in 1964, then in founding the Lowndes County Freedom Organization, whose black panther emblem later gave its name to a more famous organization. Elected SNCC chairman in 1966 in place of John Lewis, he marks a turn toward a more separatist message, viewing nonviolence as a tactic rather than a principle.

It is during the "Meredith Against Fear" march that June that he publicly launches the slogan "Black Power," breaking with the strictly integrationist, nonviolent rhetoric Martin Luther King had championed until then; he theorises this break the following year in the essay Black Power, co-written with Charles V. Hamilton. He joins the Black Panther Party in 1967, becoming its honorary prime minister in 1968, before going into exile in Guinea in 1969, under FBI and CIA surveillance, alongside the South African singer Miriam Makeba. He adopts the name Kwame Ture in 1978, honouring the African leaders Kwame Nkrumah and Ahmed Sékou Touré, and dies of prostate cancer on 15 November 1998 in Conakry, aged fifty-seven.
