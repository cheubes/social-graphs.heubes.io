A seasoned organizer, she worked for the NAACP in the 1940s and then became the SCLC's first executive director in 1957, where her horizontal style of leadership clashed with the organization's more minister-centered way of working.

In 1960, she convinced the students who had just launched the sit-in movement to form their own organization rather than join the SCLC: the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) was born at a conference she organized in Raleigh. She remained its quiet but influential mentor, advocating collective leadership rather than the cult of a single leader she felt was embodied by Martin Luther King.
