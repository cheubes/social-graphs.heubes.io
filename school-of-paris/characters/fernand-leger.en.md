Born on 4 February 1881 in Argentan, he moves to Paris at nineteen to study architecture, a plan he abandons for painting after failing the entrance exam of the École des Beaux-Arts. He shares a studio with André Mare from 1903 and settles at La Ruche, where he befriends Blaise Cendrars, Max Jacob and Guillaume Apollinaire.

Marked by a Cézanne retrospective in 1907 and then by his discovery of Picasso and Braque's Cubism, he joins the Cubist circle and exhibits at the 1911 Salon des Indépendants alongside Robert Delaunay and Juan Gris, within the group that becomes known as the Section d'Or. He develops a personal style of geometric volumes and contrasting colours, drawn to industrial and contemporary subjects rather than the intellectual abstraction of his fellow Cubists.

Marked by his experience of the First World War, he populates his canvases with mechanised bodies given equal weight to objects, as in Le Cirque Médrano (1918), and extends this aesthetic to film with Ballet mécanique in 1924. Exiled to the United States during the Second World War, where he teaches at Yale, he returns to France in 1946, joins the Communist Party and completes major religious commissions, including the stained-glass windows of the Sacré-Cœur church in Audincourt. He dies on 17 August 1955 in Gif-sur-Yvette, aged seventy-four.
