Born in 1893 in Smilovitchi, a small Jewish village near Minsk, the tenth of eleven children of a mender who earns barely two roubles a week, he shows an early passion for drawing despite religious prohibitions. Around 1909 he moves to Vilnius, where he is admitted to the School of Fine Arts in 1910 and befriends Michel Kikoïne and Pinchus Krémègne.

He moves to Paris with them on 14 July 1912 and settles at La Ruche, where he lives in extreme poverty for ten years. There he becomes a close friend of Modigliani, who teaches him manners and introduces him to his dealer Zborowski; the two men share a studio at the Cité Falguière from 1916, until Modigliani's death in 1920, which affects him deeply.

After a stay in the south of France and then Céret between 1918 and 1922, where he paints around two hundred canvases of turbulent landscapes, his expressionist painting, notably his series of beef carcasses inspired by Rembrandt's Flayed Ox (1925), finally finds an audience thanks to the collector Albert Barnes, who buys some thirty works from him at once in late 1922. A Jew hunted by the occupying forces, in hiding in Touraine, he dies on 9 August 1943 in Paris from a stomach ulcer that has plagued him all his life, aged fifty.
