Born on 27 November 1886 in Tokyo, the son of General Tsuguakira Fujita, a military physician in the Imperial Japanese Army, he receives French-language instruction from primary school and studies Western-style painting at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts, graduating in 1910. He lands in Montparnasse on 6 August 1913, dressed in a pink frock coat and a colonial helmet; the very next day, the Chilean painter Manuel Ortiz de Zárate introduces him to Picasso, an encounter that lastingly steers his art toward modernism, and he quickly befriends Modigliani, Soutine and Kisling, adopting a bohemian lifestyle at odds with his upbringing.

His 1917 exhibition of a hundred and ten watercolours brings him immediate success. He develops a unique "white background" technique blending Japanese-style ink with Western oil, paired with an extremely fine black outline, which makes his female nudes instantly recognisable and brings him great success in the 1920s.

An official painter for the Japanese army during the country's military expansion from 1938, he produces propaganda works, then, after Japan's defeat, paradoxically helps American forces gather up those same works, drawing accusations of collaboration from both sides, a wound that never fully heals. Settled in New York in 1949 and then back in Paris in 1950, he obtains French citizenship in 1955 and converts to Catholicism in 1959 under the name Léonard, in homage to Leonardo da Vinci. He dies on 29 January 1968 in Zurich, aged eighty-one.
