Born Roderic de Borja y Llançol around 1431 in Xàtiva, in the Kingdom of Valencia, the son of Jofré Borja y Llançol i Escrivà and Isabel de Borja y Cavanilles, Rodrigo Borgia studied law at the University of Bologna, graduating as one of the most eminent jurists of his day, before owing his rise to his uncle, Pope Callixtus III, who made him cardinal-deacon of San Nicola in Carcere as early as 1456, the year after his appointment as sacristan at Valencia Cathedral in 1445 and canon at three cathedrals from 1448. Appointed papal legate to Ancona and then vice-chancellor of the Holy Church in 1457, a post he held for thirty-five years, he was promoted cardinal-bishop of Albano and ordained priest in 1471, then cardinal-bishop of Porto in 1476.

Elected Archbishop of Valencia on 9 July 1492 and then pope on 11 August that year, at sixty-one, as Alexander VI, following a conclave tainted by accusations of simony, he had his Vatican apartment decorated by Pinturicchio in a lavish style that matched his own, and advanced the political fortunes of the children he had with Vannozza dei Cattanei, Cesare, born in 1475, Giovanni, Duke of Gandia, born in 1476, Lucrezia, born in 1480, and Gioffre, born in 1481, creating twelve new cardinals in 1493, including his own son Cesare, then eighteen.

He issued in 1493 the bull Inter caetera, dividing New World territories between Spain and Portugal, and established the Holy Door tradition during the 1500 Jubilee Year. His pontificate met with the constant opposition of Cardinal della Rovere, the future Julius II, who succeeded him after his death. Alexander VI died in Rome on 18 August 1503, aged seventy-two, of a fever contracted after dining with Cardinal Adriano Castellesi, and was buried at Santa Maria in Monserrato degli Spagnoli, leaving the papacy with a reputation long tarnished by the conduct of his court.
