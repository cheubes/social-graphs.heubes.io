Born Paolo Caliari in 1528 in Verona, from which he took the name by which he is known, the fifth child of Gabriele, a stonecutter, and Caterina, the illegitimate daughter of the nobleman Antonio Caliari whose surname he later adopted, he apprenticed around 1541 with Antonio Badile, his future father-in-law, and also trained under Giovanni Francesco Caroto, the two leading Veronese painters of the day; by 1544 he was no longer living with Badile, having already outgrown workshop standards. He settled in Venice in 1553, receiving state commissions for the ceilings of the Doge's Palace.

On arriving in the city, he drew on the brilliant colour of Titian, then the dominant painter of the lagoon, to develop a taste for large-scale decorative compositions, winning in 1557 a competition for the paintings of the Marciana Library judged by Titian and Sansovino, before painting between 1562 and 1563 The Wedding at Cana for the refectory of San Giorgio Maggiore, an enormous canvas measuring 677 by 994 centimetres and crowded with portraits. Married in 1565 to Elena Badile, with whom he had a daughter and four sons, he also painted between 1565 and 1570 The Family of Darius before Alexander, a rare foray into secular history painting.

Summoned on 18 July 1573 before the Venetian Holy Inquisition over the liberties taken in The Feast in the House of Levi, a canvas measuring 555 by 1,280 centimetres originally titled The Last Supper and deemed unsuitable for its German soldiers, dwarves and animals, he defended himself by arguing that "we painters take the same liberties as poets and madmen," and, on the tribunal's order, simply renamed the painting rather than alter it. A rival of Tintoretto for the major commissions of the Doge's Palace, he died in Venice on 19 April 1588, aged sixty, leaving behind an active workshop, led by his brother Benedetto and his sons Carlo and Gabriele, which continued signing works "Haeredes Pauli."
