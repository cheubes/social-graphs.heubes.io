Born Jacopo Robusti in late September or early October 1518 in Venice, the son of a dyer, Battista, from which he took his nickname (tintore), he had, according to his early biographers, only a brief apprenticeship with Titian, who is said to have angrily dismissed him after a few days; he therefore trained largely on his own, studying anatomy, drawing from models, dissecting cadavers and copying casts and prints, particularly after Michelangelo's sculptural models. He reportedly displayed in his own studio the motto "the drawing of Michelangelo and the colour of Titian", a programme he carried out through a fiery manner and dramatic lighting effects.

The Miracle of the Slave, painted in 1548 for the Scuola di San Marco, marked his breakthrough, followed in 1549 by Saint Roch Cures the Plague Victims. When Paolo Veronese arrived in Venice in 1551, he responded by offering his major works at cost, then completed between 1559 and 1560 two towering 14.5-metre canvases, the Worship of the Golden Calf and the Last Judgment, for the church of Madonna dell'Orto, before painting in 1564 three further works devoted to Saint Mark for its scuola.

He devoted much of his career, from 1565 to 1567 and then from 1575 to 1588, to decorating the Scuola Grande di San Rocco, where he painted several dozen canvases, before competing with Veronese for the major commissions of the Doge's Palace and receiving, on the latter's death in 1588, the commission for Paradise, a vast canvas measuring 9.1 by 22.6 metres, reputed to be the largest ever painted on canvas. Married around 1560, in a second marriage, to Faustina de Vescovi, with whom he had seven children, he died in Venice on 31 May 1594, aged seventy-five, of stomach pains complicated by fever, and was buried at the church of Madonna dell'Orto, beside his daughter Marietta, herself a painter, who died in 1590 aged thirty.
