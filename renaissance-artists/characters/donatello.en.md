Born Donato di Niccolò di Betto Bardi around 1386 in Florence, the son of a wool-carder belonging to the Arte della Lana guild, possibly distantly related to the powerful Bardi banking family, he first appears in the records in 1401, accused of striking a German man in Pistoia. Around 1402-1403, he travelled to Rome with his friend Brunelleschi to study ancient ruins, a trip that fed his reinvention of statuary, before training from 1404 to 1407 in Lorenzo Ghiberti's workshop, where he contributed to the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery.

He revived the classical nude and perspectival relief, the stiacciato technique, in major works such as the marble David (c. 1408-1409, reworked in 1416), the St George at Orsanmichele (1415-1417), the Zuccone on the campanile (1414-1435), and then the bronze David, the first freestanding male nude since antiquity, made between 1434 and 1440 likely at Cosimo de' Medici's request. He partnered for about nine years, from 1425, with Michelozzo in a joint workshop, collaborating notably on the tombs of antipope John XXIII and Cardinal Brancacci, before disputes, partly linked to delays on the Prato commission, ended their partnership around 1434 without breaking their friendship. He then spent ten years in Padua, where he produced from 1443 to 1453 the equestrian monument to Gattamelata, the first Renaissance bronze of its kind.

Throughout his career he enjoyed the support of Cosimo de' Medici, who continued to provide for him in old age despite his chronic inability to manage the business side of his craft. Donatello died on 13 December 1466 in Florence, aged about eighty, and, at his own request, was buried near Cosimo in the basilica of San Lorenzo, leaving behind a body of work that permanently transformed Renaissance sculpture.
