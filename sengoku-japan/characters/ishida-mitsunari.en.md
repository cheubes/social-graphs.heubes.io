Born Sakichi in 1559 in northern Omi Province, the second son of Ishida Masatsugu, a retainer of the Azai clan, his family withdrew from service after the Azai clan's defeat in 1573, before he met Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1577, then daimyo of Nagahama, and entered his service at a young age. He married Kogetsu-in, with whom he had three sons, Shigeie, Shigenari and Sakichi, and at least three daughters, including Tatsuhime.

He distinguished himself less as a warrior, though he took part in the Battle of Shizugatake in 1583 and then Komaki and Nagakute in 1584, than through his administrative and logistical skills: survey magistrate in Omi Province in 1584, administrator of Sakai in 1585, he became one of the five bugyo, the regime's top administrators, and then daimyo of Sawayama Castle in 1590, where he commanded the siege of Oshi. He took part in the invasions of Korea in 1592 as one of the three commissioners responsible for organising the campaigns, was ordered in 1597 to persecute Christians but showed them sympathy, and worked to end the war.

This rise through bureaucracy rather than the battlefield earned him the hostility of more martial generals such as Kato Kiyomasa and Fukushima Masanori, an internal rift within the Toyotomi clan that Tokugawa Ieyasu skilfully exploited after Hideyoshi's death in 1598; Mitsunari then formed an alliance against him. In 1600, he besieged Fushimi Castle before organising the Western coalition to counter Ieyasu's growing dominance, taking advantage of his departure on campaign against the Uesugi clan. The two armies met on 21 October 1600 at Sekigahara; the mid-battle defection of Kobayakawa Hideaki, on whose support he had counted, precipitated the Western army's defeat. Captured while fleeing by villagers, Mitsunari was beheaded shortly afterward in Kyoto, his head put on public display, and his remains were buried at Sangen-in, a sub-temple of Daitoku-ji.
