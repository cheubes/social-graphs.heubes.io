Born Shojumaru on 16 April 1497 at Suzuo Castle, in Aki Province, the son of Mori Hiromoto and the daughter of Fukubara Hirotoshi, he came of age in 1511 and took the name Mori Motonari, winning in 1517 the Battle of Arita-Nakaide, which established his military reputation, before becoming in July 1523, at twenty-six, full head of the Mori clan. He married around 1522 the daughter of Kikkawa Kunitsune, with whom he had three sons who would form the core of the "Two Rivers," the eldest Mori Takamoto, then Kikkawa Motoharu and Kobayakawa Takakage, adopted respectively into the Kikkawa and Kobayakawa houses; he fathered nine sons and three daughters in total through several wives and concubines.

Starting from a modest domain, he made the Mori clan the dominant power in western Japan through a combination of diplomatic cunning and military victories: after switching allegiance from the Amago to the Ouchi in 1525, he successfully defended Yoshida-Koriyama Castle in 1540-1541 against Amago Haruhisa's siege. When Sue Takafusa assassinated Ouchi Yoshitaka in 1551, he rose in revolt and won a decisive victory in October 1555 at the Battle of Miyajima, destroying Sue's army through a naval manoeuvre and a stratagem carried out at night in a driving thunderstorm. He then invaded Izumo Province in 1562, leading the second siege of Toda Castle from 1562 to 1563, where Amago Yoshihisa's execution of his own retainer Moriyama Hisakane triggered desertions that forced the clan's surrender.

Anxious to secure his legacy's cohesion, he placed his two younger sons, Kikkawa Motoharu, who controlled Iwami Province and the northern territories, and Kobayakawa Takakage, who dominated Bingo Province and the naval forces, at the head of allied houses rather than let them contest power with each other, a system known as the "Two Rivers" that effectively backed the eldest son who remained head of the clan. He died on 14 June 1571 at Yoshida-Koriyama Castle, aged seventy-four, of oesophageal cancer combined with old age, before the great upheavals of unification, leaving a solid domain that his successors would later have to reckon with under Oda Nobunaga and then Toyotomi Hideyoshi.
