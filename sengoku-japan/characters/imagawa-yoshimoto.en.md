Born in 1519 in Sunpu, Suruga Province, the son of Imagawa Ujichika and Jukei-ni, a third son first sent to a temple under the name Baigaku Shoho, he unexpectedly inherited leadership of the clan in 1536 on the death of his elder brother Ujiteru, securing his position by defeating his half-brother Genko Etan in the Hanagura Disturbance, with the support of the Hojo and Takeda clans. He married Jokei-in, with whom he had five children including his successor Imagawa Ujizane, and further cemented these alliances through the marriages of his brothers-in-law Takeda Shingen and Hojo Ujiyasu.

The leader of one of the most powerful and refined clans of central Japan, he extended his influence between 1536 and 1560 over Suruga, Totomi and Mikawa provinces, suffering a defeat at Azukizaka in 1542 before avenging it in 1548, and holding among others the young Matsudaira Motoyasu, the future Tokugawa Ieyasu, as a hostage and then a vassal at his court. Around 1554, his expansion continued westward, where it soon met the growing resistance of Oda Nobunaga.

On 12 June 1560, at the head of an army of roughly twenty-five thousand men, he marched toward Kyoto through Oda Nobunaga's territory. Confident in his numerical superiority, he let his troops relax, singing and drinking sake during a halt at Okehazama, where Nobunaga, with only three thousand men, surprised him with a lightning attack; two Oda samurai killed Yoshimoto in the village of Dengakuhazama. He died at forty or forty-one and was buried at the Daisei-ji temple in Toyokawa; this unexpected defeat shattered the Imagawa clan's power at a stroke and freed Ieyasu, who immediately broke his allegiance to ally with Nobunaga instead.
